# Hand-Knotted vs. Hand-Tufted vs. Machine-Made: 7 Things to Check Before You Buy

**Last Updated: October 12, 2026**

## Quick answer

A **hand-knotted rug** is made by tying individual knots, one at a time, onto the vertical warp threads of a loom. It's the slowest and most durable way to make a rug. A **hand-tufted rug** is made by punching yarn loops through a canvas backing with a handheld tool and then gluing a second backing on. A **machine-made rug** is produced on a power loom, or printed. All three can look alike in a product photo, but they differ a lot in construction, lifespan, and value.

## The 7 things to check

### 1. Turn the rug over and look at the back
This is the most reliable test. On a hand-knotted rug the design shows clearly on the back, almost as a mirror image of the front, and you can see the individual knots. Small variations in knot size and spacing are normal. If the back is covered by canvas, felt, or a smooth fabric layer, the rug is almost certainly hand-tufted or machine-made.

### 2. Check where the fringe comes from
On a hand-knotted rug the fringe is the ends of the warp threads, the foundation the knots were tied onto, so it runs right through the rug. On most tufted and machine-made rugs the fringe is a separate strip sewn or glued onto the ends for looks.

### 3. Look for a glued canvas backing
Hand-tufted rugs are held together by latex glue and a backing cloth, not by knots. The backing is often the giveaway. Over time the glue can dry out and break down, which leads to shedding and loose tufts. That's the main reason tufted rugs usually don't last as long as hand-knotted ones.

### 4. Treat small irregularities as a sign of handwork
A hand-knotted rug often has slight variations in its lines, borders, or color, especially in older or vintage pieces. These come from a weaver tying each knot by hand and aren't flaws. A perfectly uniform, repeating pattern points to machine production.

### 5. Ask what fiber it's made from
Hand-knotted rugs are traditionally made from natural fibers. Wool is the most common pile fiber because it's resilient and takes dye well. Silk or wool-silk blends add sheen, and cotton is often used for the foundation (warp and weft). Across Solo Rugs' listings the main fiber is wool, and wool-silk blends and cotton also appear.

### 6. Compare value over the rug's lifetime, not just the price tag
Hand-knotted rugs cost more because each one takes skilled weavers months to finish. The trade-off is lifespan: a well-made hand-knotted rug can last for decades and often keeps its value, while tufted and machine-made rugs are usually replaced much sooner. Hand-knotting is what makes a truly one-of-a-kind rug possible. Each piece is tied individually, so no two come out exactly the same. Machine-made rugs are designed to repeat. ## Common questions

**Is a hand-knotted rug worth the price?**
For a rug you want to keep for many years, often yes. Hand-knotted construction lasts much longer than glued, tufted construction, and a unique piece can't be replaced with an identical copy. If you need a short-term or low-cost option, a tufted or machine-made rug may suit you better.

**What's the difference between hand-knotted and hand-loomed (or handwoven)?**
"Handwoven" is a broad term that covers any rug made by hand on a loom. Hand-knotted is the specific technique where a knot is tied around the warp threads for every point of pile. Flatweaves such as kilims are also handwoven, but they have no knotted pile.
